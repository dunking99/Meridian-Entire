The Rotation, Interrupted

Energy and utilities carried a defensive tape to its worst week since November, as the Fed held its fire and the long end finally found a buyer.

The index closed at 6,528.44, but the number flatters the session. Beneath the headline, 301 stocks fell against 196 that rose, and the median member of the benchmark lost 0.58% — a wider decline than the one recorded at the top of the tape. Eleven of the last twenty sessions have produced more new lows than new highs.

The cause was not one thing. A Federal Reserve that declined to cut, a thirty-year auction that cleared without drama, and a technology group still digesting four weeks of earnings guidance all pointed the same way: out of the fastest-moving parts of the market and into utilities, health care and crude oil.

Energy was the only sector positive across every window the desk measures — the day, the week, the month and the year. That has happened eleven times since 2011; nine of those occasions preceded a two-month drawdown in the broader market.

Elsewhere the rotation was unmistakable. Small caps fell 1.14% against large-cap value's 0.42% gain, junk bond spreads widened six basis points, and the put/call ratio closed above 1.10 for the fourth time in a fortnight.

There is a version of this tape in which the market is simply taking a breather. The index fell 0.42%, technology gave back a little of a very large run, and crude oil — which has its own reasons — carried everything from pipelines to refiners higher. On that reading, very little happened.

The harder version starts with the component data. Thirty-one of the fifty largest companies in the benchmark fell. The median stock in the index fell more than the index did, which means the largest names are still absorbing the decline on everyone else's behalf. Twelve sectors exist; the top three and the bottom three moved in opposite directions for the fourth time this month.

Rotation of this speed is not a sign of health so much as a sign of searching. Money is leaving the crowded trade, but it has not settled anywhere: cash ETFs took in another $6.2bn yesterday, the fourteenth consecutive session of inflows, and that money is not yet visible in bonds, gold or equities.

What would settle it? Either a fresh catalyst in technology — the next earnings cycle begins in three weeks — or a genuine deterioration in the labour data. Until one arrives, the market is likely to keep rotating faster than it travels.

Dollar, three months

DXY
Yen weakness is doing most of the work: 61% of the index's monthly gain comes from a single cross.

A steepening curve is the bond market's way of saying it no longer believes the disinflation story is the whole story. Two-year yields rose six basis points because the committee declined to cut; thirty-year yields fell two basis points because an auction that everyone feared went fine. Those two facts are not contradictory — they are the market separating the next six months from the next six years.

The dollar behaved exactly as the rate sheet says it should, adding 0.28% and taking its monthly gain past 1.2%. The exception, as ever lately, is the yen. Japan's currency is 3.2% stronger over the month even as its two-year yield gap with the United States barely moved — a reminder that intervention risk and repatriation flows do not appear in any interest-rate model.

Watch the breakevens. Ten-year inflation expectations finished at 2.54%, a basis point higher on the day and eleven higher on the month. If the market starts pricing 2.75% again, the Fed's two-cut path disappears faster than the dot plot can defend it — and gold, at 96th percentile of its year, will be the first asset to notice.

Every commodity market is currently answering a different question. Crude is asking whether supply will be there in June and answering, provisionally, that it will not be needed. Gold is asking what central banks know, and answering that they have been buying for eleven straight months. Copper is asking about China's construction cycle and getting no reply at all.

The gold premium is the interesting number. Strip out the real yield relationship that governed the metal for a decade and it is $366 too expensive — 3.2 standard deviations of residual. Central bank purchases explain roughly half of that; reserve diversification and ETF inflows explain most of the rest. None of it is speculative flow, which is precisely why the premium has not unwound.

Copper, by contrast, is cheap on every measure the desk runs: the 14th percentile of its range, a negative roll yield, and a Baltic Dry index down 38% on the year. Freight rates lead trade volumes by about two quarters, and the freight tape is currently forecasting a contraction the official data has not yet printed.

The trade that reconciles the two is the oldest one in the building: buy the metal that central banks buy, sell the one that needs Chinese demand, and wait for somebody to be proved wrong.
